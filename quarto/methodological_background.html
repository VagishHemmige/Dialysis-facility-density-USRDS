Methodological background

This page explains, in general terms and without code, the data and methods a study like this one rests on: what the United States Renal Data System and the Medical Evidence form are, how a patient is placed in a health care area, where area-level information comes from, and why patients grouped in areas need particular models. What this study chose is on Study design and methods.

The United States Renal Data System

The United States Renal Data System (USRDS) is a national data system on chronic kidney disease and kidney failure in the United States, funded by the National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases (United States Renal Data System 2024). Researchers receive it as a set of files linked by one patient identifier. Two matter most for studying how dialysis begins:

  • the Medical Evidence file, which holds the forms completed when a person starts treatment for kidney failure;
  • the Residence file, which holds a person’s residential ZIP code and state as a series of periods with start and end dates (United States Renal Data System 2024).

The meaning of each coded value is given by a named list of codes (a format) in the USRDS Researcher’s Guide (United States Renal Data System 2024). Formats can change between releases, so code written for one release needs checking against the formats of the release it runs on.

The Medical Evidence form

The CMS-2728 End Stage Renal Disease Medical Evidence Report is completed when a person starts regular dialysis or receives a kidney transplant, including a transplant instead of a course of dialysis (Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services 2004, 2015a; United States Renal Data System 2024). It records, at that moment, demographics (age, sex, race, Hispanic ethnicity); coexisting conditions as yes/no fields; laboratory values before the first dialysis, including creatinine, albumin and hemoglobin; the type and setting of dialysis, the vascular access and insurance; whether the patient saw a nephrologist beforehand; and the primary cause of kidney failure, as a code from a list CMS publishes (Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services 2015b).

Coexisting conditions are those the facility recorded, not confirmed by separate review. When checked against clinical records, the form’s conditions were often missing where present (average sensitivity 0.59) but rarely recorded where absent (specificity above 0.95) (Longenecker et al. 2000). A person can have more than one form, for example after recovering kidney function and later restarting dialysis, so studies of the first start use the earliest.

Estimated GFR at the start of dialysis

The glomerular filtration rate (GFR) measures how much blood the kidneys filter each minute. It is rarely measured directly; in practice it is estimated (eGFR) from serum creatinine with an equation, in mL/min/1.73 m² (adjusted to a standard body size) (Stevens et al. 2006). Creatinine results depend on the laboratory method; standardizing them across laboratories was recommended in 2006 (Myers et al. 2006). The CKD-EPI equation published in 2009 uses creatinine, age, sex and race, and gives a higher eGFR to a patient recorded as Black than to another with the same creatinine (Levey et al. 2009). Equations published in 2021 leave race out (Inker et al. 2021), and US nephrology societies recommend them (Delgado et al. 2022).

The eGFR at the start of dialysis describes when in the course of kidney disease dialysis began; a start at a higher eGFR is often called an early start. The IDEAL trial randomized adults to start dialysis at a higher or a lower estimated GFR and found no survival benefit from starting earlier (Cooper et al. 2010); IDEAL estimated GFR with the Cockcroft-Gault equation, not CKD-EPI, so its thresholds do not carry over exactly to CKD-EPI values. Studies have asked what, besides the patient’s kidney function, influences when dialysis starts, including how much it varies between areas and how local care is organized (O’Hare et al. 2010; Scialla et al. 2014; Li et al. 2017; Hemmige et al. 2024). Most of that variation remains unexplained by recorded patient and physician characteristics (Li et al. 2017). The form records a creatinine value from the 45 days before the first dialysis (Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services 2015a), so the eGFR at initiation approximates, rather than records, the value clinicians saw when they decided to start.

Dialysis facility listings

The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) publishes lists of dialysis facilities, with each facility’s ZIP code, stations and ownership, and archives earlier releases (Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services 2025). Each release describes the facilities at that time, so the facilities near a patient on a given date come from the release current on that date.

Hospital Service Areas

The Dartmouth Atlas of Health Care divides the United States into Hospital Service Areas (HSAs): groups of ZIP codes whose residents receive most of their hospital care locally (The Dartmouth Atlas of Health Care, n.d.-a). Each ZIP code is assigned to the area where the largest share of its residents’ hospital stays took place, which need not be a majority (The Dartmouth Atlas of Health Care, n.d.-c). An HSA is a local health care market rather than a political boundary. The Atlas publishes a crosswalk assigning each ZIP code to an HSA for each year, because ZIP codes are added and retired (The Dartmouth Atlas of Health Care, n.d.-e).

The Atlas also publishes measures for each HSA, such as the share of Medicare enrollees with diabetes who receive eye examinations and HbA1c testing, the rate of hospital discharges for conditions that good outpatient care can often prevent (ambulatory-sensitive discharges), and hospital beds and staff per resident (The Dartmouth Atlas of Health Care, n.d.-d, n.d.-b). Beds and staff are allocated to HSAs by where each hospital’s Medicare patients live, not by where the hospital stands, and are expressed per resident of all ages (The Dartmouth Atlas of Health Care, n.d.-c). Values too small to be reliable, and values suppressed, are stored as special negative codes; these are flags, not numbers, and are treated as missing (The Dartmouth Atlas of Health Care, n.d.-d).

ZIP codes and ZCTAs

ZIP codes are mail delivery routes, not areas with fixed boundaries, and they are added, changed and retired (Grubesic and Matisziw 2006). The Census Bureau publishes its statistics for ZIP Code Tabulation Areas (ZCTAs), areas built to approximate the territory of ZIP codes (U.S. Census Bureau, n.d.-b). Most ZIP codes have a ZCTA with the same number, but not every ZIP code has one (U.S. Census Bureau, n.d.-b); a ZIP code with no territory of its own, such as one used only for post office boxes, is counted within a surrounding ZCTA (Grubesic and Matisziw 2006).

Linking a ZIP code to Census data therefore needs a crosswalk from ZIP code to ZCTA. The UDS Mapper published one for each year, and the zippeR R package makes them available (UDS Mapper, n.d.; Prener et al. 2026). Matching by number alone loses the people living in ZIP codes whose ZCTA has a different number.

American Community Survey estimates

The American Community Survey (ACS) is a continuous survey run by the Census Bureau. For small areas such as ZCTAs it publishes 5-year estimates, which pool 60 months of responses; an estimate labelled with a year describes the five years ending in that year, not that year alone (U.S. Census Bureau 2020). A subject table gathers many characteristics of an area (population, race, income, poverty) in one place (U.S. Census Bureau 2020). The tidycensus R package downloads ACS tables (Walker and Herman 2026). The Census Bureau changes tables between releases (U.S. Census Bureau, n.d.-a), so the codes that identify variables can change, and each year’s labels need checking.

Rural-urban commuting area codes

The US Department of Agriculture’s Economic Research Service publishes Rural-Urban Commuting Area (RUCA) codes, which classify areas by population density, urbanization and daily commuting (USDA Economic Research Service 2020). Its ZIP code file gives each ZIP code a primary code, from metropolitan core to rural, with a separate code for ZIP codes with no population. The primary codes are commonly grouped into metropolitan, micropolitan, small town and rural areas (USDA Economic Research Service 2020, n.d.).

Area-level exposures

An area-level exposure describes where a person lives, not the person: for example, dialysis facilities per resident of an HSA. Everyone in the same area has the same value. Such exposures suit questions about how care is organized locally, but need care in interpretation:

  • An association between an area’s characteristics and its residents’ outcomes describes people living in such areas. It does not show which individual patients were affected, or how; inferring individual relationships from area patterns is known as the ecological fallacy (Piantadosi et al. 1988; Morgenstern 1995).
  • Results can depend on how areas are drawn: a measure computed for HSAs may behave differently for counties. This is known as the modifiable areal unit problem (Fotheringham and Wong 1991).
  • A rate per resident needs a population for the same area and time. Where the area is built from smaller units, each unit’s population should be counted once.

Such studies are observational: they describe associations, adjusted for measured differences, and cannot by themselves show that changing the exposure would change the outcome.

Mixed-effects logistic regression

Logistic regression models a yes/no outcome, such as starting dialysis at or above an eGFR threshold. Results are reported as odds ratios: how much higher or lower the odds of the outcome are in one group than in a reference group, with the other variables in the model held fixed. Adding groups of variables in stages shows how the estimate changes as each group is added. Because odds ratios are not collapsible, a change between models is not the share of the association that the added variables explain, and the coefficients of the adjustment variables are not estimates of their own effects (Greenland et al. 1999; Westreich and Greenland 2013).

Standard logistic regression assumes each patient’s outcome is independent of every other’s. Patients in the same area share nephrologists, hospitals and local habits of practice. A mixed-effects (or multilevel) model allows for this with a random intercept for each area, a term that lets the baseline odds differ from area to area. Ignoring the grouping would make confidence intervals too narrow, especially for an exposure measured at the area level (Moulton 1990; Austin and Merlo 2017).

Fitting a mixed model requires averaging over every area’s random intercept, which must be approximated. The Laplace approximation uses one point per area; adaptive Gauss-Hermite quadrature uses several, and is more exact but slower (Pinheiro and Bates 1995). Stata’s mixed-effects logistic command uses adaptive quadrature by default (StataCorp, n.d.); the R packages lme4 and glmmTMB fit the same models (Bates et al. 2015; Brooks et al. 2017). For the fixed effects, such as an exposure’s odds ratio, the Laplace approximation’s bias shrinks as the number of patients per area grows, so with large areas the two give practically the same answer (Joe 2008); with yes/no outcomes in small areas, or for the size of the variation between areas, they can differ (Joe 2008; StataCorp, n.d.). The same structure with a continuous outcome is a linear mixed model.

How much variation lies between areas

A null linear mixed model, with no explanatory variables, divides the variation of a continuous outcome such as eGFR into a part between areas and a part between patients within areas; the share between areas is the intraclass correlation (Merlo et al. 2006; Austin and Merlo 2017). In R it can be fitted with lme4 (Bates et al. 2015). Groupings can be nested, such as dialysis facilities within HSAs, to show the share at each level. Shares from different models are comparable only when the models use the same patients.

Missing laboratory values

A complete-case analysis uses only patients with every value recorded; it loses patients and can be biased if they differ systematically. Multiple imputation fills in each missing value several times from a model of the other variables, analyses each completed data set, and combines the results by Rubin’s rules, so the uncertainty about the missing values is carried into the confidence intervals (Rubin 1987; Buuren and Groothuis-Oudshoorn 2011). Chained equations impute each variable in turn; predictive mean matching fills each gap with a value observed in a similar patient (Buuren and Groothuis-Oudshoorn 2011; White et al. 2011).

Reporting standards

STROBE is a checklist for reporting observational studies, including a flow diagram of exclusions (Elm et al. 2007). RECORD extends it to routinely collected data such as registries, adding the codes and algorithms that define variables, how sources were linked, and access to data and code (Benchimol et al. 2015).

The Atlas publishes a crosswalk assigning each ZIP code to an HSA for each year, because ZIP codes are added and retired [@dartmouth_crosswalks].

The Atlas also publishes measures for each HSA, such as the share of Medicare enrollees with diabetes who receive eye examinations and HbA1c testing, the rate of hospital discharges for conditions that good outpatient care can often prevent (ambulatory-sensitive discharges), and hospital beds and staff per resident [@dartmouth_primarycare; @dartmouth_capacity]. Beds and staff are allocated to HSAs by where each hospital's Medicare patients live, not by where the hospital stands, and are expressed per resident of all ages [@dartmouth_methods]. Values too small to be reliable, and values suppressed, are stored as special negative codes; these are flags, not numbers, and are treated as missing [@dartmouth_primarycare].

## ZIP codes and ZCTAs

ZIP codes are mail delivery routes, not areas with fixed boundaries, and they are added, changed and retired [@grubesic2006zcta]. The Census Bureau publishes its statistics for ZIP Code Tabulation Areas (ZCTAs), areas built to approximate the territory of ZIP codes [@census_zcta]. Most ZIP codes have a ZCTA with the same number, but not every ZIP code has one [@census_zcta]; a ZIP code with no territory of its own, such as one used only for post office boxes, is counted within a surrounding ZCTA [@grubesic2006zcta].

Linking a ZIP code to Census data therefore needs a crosswalk from ZIP code to ZCTA. The UDS Mapper published one for each year, and the `zippeR` R package makes them available [@uds_crosswalk; @zipper_pkg]. Matching by number alone loses the people living in ZIP codes whose ZCTA has a different number.

## American Community Survey estimates

The American Community Survey (ACS) is a continuous survey run by the Census Bureau. For small areas such as ZCTAs it publishes *5-year estimates*, which pool 60 months of responses; an estimate labelled with a year describes the five years ending in that year, not that year alone [@census2020acshandbook]. A *subject table* gathers many characteristics of an area (population, race, income, poverty) in one place [@census2020acshandbook]. The `tidycensus` R package downloads ACS tables [@tidycensus]. The Census Bureau changes tables between releases [@census_acs_tablechanges], so the codes that identify variables can change, and each year's labels need checking.

## Rural-urban commuting area codes

The US Department of Agriculture's Economic Research Service publishes Rural-Urban Commuting Area (RUCA) codes, which classify areas by population density, urbanization and daily commuting [@ers2020ruca]. Its ZIP code file gives each ZIP code a primary code, from metropolitan core to rural, with a separate code for ZIP codes with no population. The primary codes are commonly grouped into metropolitan, micropolitan, small town and rural areas [@ers2020ruca; @ers_ruca_documentation].

## Area-level exposures

An *area-level* exposure describes where a person lives, not the person: for example, dialysis facilities per resident of an HSA. Everyone in the same area has the same value. Such exposures suit questions about how care is organized locally, but need care in interpretation:

-   An association between an area's characteristics and its residents' outcomes describes people living in such areas. It does not show which individual patients were affected, or how; inferring individual relationships from area patterns is known as the *ecological fallacy* [@piantadosi1988ecological; @morgenstern1995ecologic].
-   Results can depend on how areas are drawn: a measure computed for HSAs may behave differently for counties. This is known as the *modifiable areal unit problem* [@fotheringham1991maup].
-   A rate per resident needs a population for the same area and time. Where the area is built from smaller units, each unit's population should be counted once.

Such studies are observational: they describe associations, adjusted for measured differences, and cannot by themselves show that changing the exposure would change the outcome.

## Mixed-effects logistic regression

Logistic regression models a yes/no outcome, such as starting dialysis at or above an eGFR threshold. Results are reported as **odds ratios**: how much higher or lower the odds of the outcome are in one group than in a reference group, with the other variables in the model held fixed. Adding groups of variables in stages shows how the estimate changes as each group is added. Because odds ratios are not *collapsible*, a change between models is not the share of the association that the added variables explain, and the coefficients of the adjustment variables are not estimates of their own effects [@greenland1999collapsibility; @westreich2013table2].

Standard logistic regression assumes each patient's outcome is independent of every other's. Patients in the same area share nephrologists, hospitals and local habits of practice. A **mixed-effects** (or *multilevel*) model allows for this with a **random intercept** for each area, a term that lets the baseline odds differ from area to area. Ignoring the grouping would make confidence intervals too narrow, especially for an exposure measured at the area level [@moulton1990pitfall; @austin2017multilevel].

Fitting a mixed model requires averaging over every area's random intercept, which must be approximated. The Laplace approximation uses one point per area; adaptive Gauss-Hermite quadrature uses several, and is more exact but slower [@pinheiro1995approx]. Stata's mixed-effects logistic command uses adaptive quadrature by default [@stata_melogit]; the R packages `lme4` and `glmmTMB` fit the same models [@lme4_pkg; @glmmtmb]. For the fixed effects, such as an exposure's odds ratio, the Laplace approximation's bias shrinks as the number of patients per area grows, so with large areas the two give practically the same answer [@joe2008laplace]; with yes/no outcomes in small areas, or for the size of the variation between areas, they can differ [@joe2008laplace; @stata_melogit]. The same structure with a continuous outcome is a linear mixed model.

## How much variation lies between areas

A *null* linear mixed model, with no explanatory variables, divides the variation of a continuous outcome such as eGFR into a part between areas and a part between patients within areas; the share between areas is the intraclass correlation [@merlo2006tutorial; @austin2017multilevel]. In R it can be fitted with `lme4` [@lme4_pkg]. Groupings can be nested, such as dialysis facilities within HSAs, to show the share at each level. Shares from different models are comparable only when the models use the same patients.

## Missing laboratory values

A **complete-case** analysis uses only patients with every value recorded; it loses patients and can be biased if they differ systematically. **Multiple imputation** fills in each missing value several times from a model of the other variables, analyses each completed data set, and combines the results by Rubin's rules, so the uncertainty about the missing values is carried into the confidence intervals [@rubin1987mi; @vanbuuren2011mice]. *Chained equations* impute each variable in turn; *predictive mean matching* fills each gap with a value observed in a similar patient [@vanbuuren2011mice; @white2011mice].

## Reporting standards

STROBE is a checklist for reporting observational studies, including a flow diagram of exclusions [@vonelm2007strobe]. RECORD extends it to routinely collected data such as registries, adding the codes and algorithms that define variables, how sources were linked, and access to data and code [@benchimol2015record].
