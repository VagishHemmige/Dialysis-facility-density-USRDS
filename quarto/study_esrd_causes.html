Mapping cause codes with ellmer

Author
Affiliation

Vagish Hemmige

MAHV

This page is part of the study’s design and methods. The Specified in and Current code notes are explained on the Overview, and the general background is on Methodological background. The code that carries this out is on 2. Mapping cause codes with ellmer.

The paper’s Table 1 groups each patient’s primary cause of kidney failure into six categories. The original analysis took the grouping from a spreadsheet that no longer exists. The replication rebuilds it by asking three cloud large language models, from three companies, to classify every USRDS cause code, and accepting a code’s category when at least two of the three agree. Two smaller models that run on the analyst’s own computer were asked the same question and are reported for comparison, but do not vote. Only the public list of codes and their descriptions is sent to any model, never patient data.

The mapping from the first full run, on 30 October 2026, is accepted for the replication as the models drafted it (decided by Vagish Hemmige, 30 October 2026; decision D4): 423 of the 445 codes were unanimous and 22 accepted by two of three, none were left for review, and the deciders changed none.

Specified in: §6 Covariates (“Cause of kidney failure mapping”).

The code list

The original mapping spreadsheet is lost. Its replacement starts from a public list: every code of USRDS format $PD96DET with its description, from Appendix C of the Researcher’s Guide (United States Renal Data System 2024), which usRds already holds. There are 445 codes: 128 ICD-10-CM codes from the 2015 form, and 317 older codes, mostly ICD-9-CM codes (many with a letter added by USRDS) plus a few that mean the cause is missing. No patient data are involved, and the list is not filtered by what appears in the data. (The original spreadsheet was built from the codes that appeared in the data.)

The prompt

All five models, voting or not, receive the same prompt, word for word. It is kept as a text file, prompts/esrd-cause-classification.md, so that it can be read and edited on its own and shown here exactly as sent:

You are a nephrologist classifying the primary cause of kidney failure recorded on the CMS-2728 Medical Evidence form, using the USRDS code list.

Assign each code to exactly one of these categories:

- Diabetes: kidney failure attributed to type 1 or type 2 diabetes, including diabetic nephropathy and diabetes with renal manifestations.
- Hypertension and cardiac disease: hypertensive kidney disease and nephrosclerosis, hypertensive heart and kidney disease, renovascular and other large-vessel disease of the kidney (renal artery stenosis, occlusion or thrombosis, renal atheroembolism), and kidney failure attributed to heart disease, such as cardiorenal syndrome or heart failure.
- Glomerulonephritis: primary and secondary glomerular disease. Primary includes IgA nephropathy, focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, membranous nephropathy, membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis, and acute, rapidly progressive or chronic nephritic and nephrotic syndromes. Secondary includes glomerulonephritis caused by systemic disease: lupus nephritis, ANCA-associated vasculitis (granulomatosis with polyangiitis or Wegener's granulomatosis, microscopic polyangiitis, polyarteritis), anti-GBM disease (Goodpasture's syndrome) and Henoch-Schönlein purpura. It also includes infection-related glomerular disease: post-infectious glomerulonephritis, HIV-associated (AIDS) nephropathy, glomerulonephritis associated with hepatitis B or C, and cryoglobulinemic glomerulonephritis. Thrombotic microangiopathies (hemolytic-uremic syndrome, thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura), scleroderma, and lupus described as tubulointerstitial disease are not in this category.
- Hereditary or polycystic kidney disease: polycystic kidney disease and other inherited kidney diseases, including medullary cystic disease, Alport syndrome, Fabry disease, cystinosis, primary oxalosis and tuberous sclerosis. Congenital anomalies that are not described as inherited (renal hypoplasia or dysplasia, congenital obstruction) are not in this category.
- Transplant failure or complications: complications or failure of any transplanted solid organ or of bone marrow.
- Other: any cause that fits none of the five categories above. This includes:
  - other kidney and urinary tract disease: interstitial nephritis, pyelonephritis and other kidney or urinary tract infections (including renal tuberculosis), obstructive and urologic disease, congenital anomalies that are not inherited, and acute kidney injury;
  - systemic disease that is not glomerulonephritis: thrombotic microangiopathies, scleroderma, neoplasms (including myeloma and amyloidosis);
  - codes meaning that the cause is missing, unknown or unreconcilable.

Classify from the code and its description only; do not assume information that is not given.
Return one entry per code, with the code exactly as given and a reason of at most 15 words.

Codes:
{{code_list}}

For each batch, {code_list} becomes one line per code: the code, a colon and the USRDS description from the Researcher’s Guide (for example, 4464Z: WEGENERS GRANULOMATOSIS). Nothing else about a code is sent.

The prompt makes three rules:

  • One category per code, from the six in the paper’s Table 1, each defined in words.
  • “Other” is the catch-all, for every cause outside the first five and for codes that mean missing, unknown or unreconcilable. This is an assumption: the original spreadsheet had a sheet named “Other”, but what it contained is not known.
  • No inference beyond the description. The model classifies from the code and its description only, which keeps it from inventing context.

The six categories

The original analysis took the six categories from a spreadsheet with one sheet per category (“Diabetes”, “Hypertensive and Cardiac”, “Glomerulonephritis”, “HereditaryPCKD”, “Transplant”, “Other”), which no longer exists. Only the categories’ names survive, in the sheet names and in the paper’s Table 1, which labels the transplant category “solid organ and bone marrow” (Hemmige et al. 2024).

The definitions in the prompt (decided by Vagish Hemmige, 27 October 2026) start from the 12 headings of the 2015 CMS-2728 list of primary causes (Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services 2015) and depart from them where the clinical definition differs:

CMS-2728 heading (2015) Category here
Diabetes Diabetes
Glomerulonephritis Glomerulonephritis
Secondary Glomerulonephritis/Vasculitis Glomerulonephritis for lupus nephritis, ANCA vasculitis and Henoch-Schönlein purpura; Other for thrombotic microangiopathies (hemolytic-uremic syndrome, thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura) and scleroderma
Interstitial Nephritis/Pyelonephritis Other
Transplant Complications Transplant failure or complications
Hypertension/Large Vessel Disease Hypertension and cardiac disease
Cystic/Hereditary/Congenital/Other Diseases Hereditary or polycystic kidney disease for cystic and inherited disease; Other for congenital anomalies not described as inherited
Neoplasms/Tumors Other
Disorders of Mineral Metabolism Other
Genitourinary System Other
Acute Kidney Failure Other
Miscellaneous Conditions Other, except heart failure (Hypertension and cardiac disease) and HIV disease (Glomerulonephritis)

The 2015 list covers only the ICD-10-CM codes. The older ICD-9-CM codes in $PD96DET are placed by the same definitions; anti-GBM disease (Goodpasture’s syndrome), for example, appears only among them and counts as glomerulonephritis.

Four departures need a reason:

  • Secondary glomerulonephritis is glomerulonephritis. The CMS list keeps it apart, but lupus nephritis, ANCA vasculitis and Henoch-Schönlein purpura are glomerular inflammation. Lupus described as tubulointerstitial disease is not, and goes to Other. Thrombotic microangiopathies and scleroderma kidney disease, in the same CMS heading, are injury to the small vessels and endothelium rather than glomerular inflammation, so they also go to Other.
  • Infection-related glomerular disease is glomerulonephritis: post-infectious glomerulonephritis, glomerulonephritis with hepatitis B or C, and cryoglobulinemic glomerulonephritis. Infections that are not glomerular, such as pyelonephritis and renal tuberculosis, stay in Other.
  • HIV-associated nephropathy is glomerulonephritis. The 2015 CMS list places HIV disease (code B20) under Miscellaneous Conditions (Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services 2015). Clinically, HIV-associated nephropathy is a glomerular disease whose classic form is collapsing focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (Rosenberg et al. 2015), so here it counts as glomerulonephritis, a deliberate departure from the CMS grouping. On the first run, before the definitions were written, all three models put HIV in Other. Earlier versions of the prompt are in the repository’s history, and each saved answer file names the prompt version it answered.
  • Heart failure is cardiac disease. The sheet name “Hypertensive and Cardiac” suggests that kidney failure attributed to heart disease belonged with hypertension, so heart failure (I50.9, in Miscellaneous Conditions on the CMS list) counts there.

Transplant failure or complications. The paper’s Table 1 labels this category “solid organ and bone marrow”. On a first Medical Evidence form it can mean kidney failure after a transplant of another organ (heart, liver, lung) or of bone marrow, for example from calcineurin-inhibitor toxicity (Ojo et al. 2003). A patient whose kidney transplant fails already had kidney failure before that transplant, so their first form would usually date from then rather than from the failed transplant.

What the paper’s Table 1 can and cannot show. In the published cohort, the six categories were diabetes 48.0%, hypertension and cardiac disease 29.5%, glomerulonephritis 5.7%, hereditary or polycystic kidney disease 1.9%, transplant 0.5% and other 14.3% (computed from the Table 1 counts). Among all incident patients in 2016, glomerulonephritis was 7.3% and cystic kidney disease 2.8% (computed from the counts in Table 1.6 of the 2018 USRDS Annual Data Report (United States Renal Data System 2018)). The cohort is in-center hemodialysis only, and patients with glomerulonephritis or cystic kidney disease more often start on peritoneal dialysis or receive a transplant first (Table 1.6 (United States Renal Data System 2018)), so these percentages cannot show where the original spreadsheet drew either boundary. Small groups such as HIV are invisible in these percentages.

Which models, and how they are asked

All five models are called from R through the ellmer package (Wickham et al. 2026), which gives one interface to each provider’s model and to models run locally. Each is asked for structured output: a reply in a fixed form, one category and a short reason per code, in which the category can only be one of the six names. A model therefore cannot invent a seventh category, and every answer can be checked mechanically: each code answered exactly once, no codes that are not in the list. The codes are sent in batches, each in a new conversation, so one batch’s answers cannot influence the next. No temperature or seed is set, so a second run could give different answers; the saved answer files are the record of what each model said.

The three cloud models are each provider’s current general-purpose model. The two local models run through Ollama on the analyst’s computer (decided by Vagish Hemmige, 28 October 2026): Alibaba’s Qwen and Meta’s Llama, from two further companies. They were chosen to vote, and were moved to reporting only on 29 October 2026 (decided by Vagish Hemmige), when neither produced a complete, valid set of answers. Llama 3.1 replaced IBM’s Granite (granite4.2:8b) on 29 October 2026 (decided by Vagish Hemmige): on the analyst’s laptop Granite timed out after 30 minutes on a batch of 25 codes, and returned no answers for 5 codes in 15 minutes. Ollama’s models trained for medicine were considered, but they are all Google’s MedGemma, which would overlap with Gemini, or older models built on Llama 2. Both local models are small enough to run on a laptop. They show the approach that would be needed if a classification ever involved patient data: a model on the analyst’s own computer sends nothing to an outside company. Here only the public code list is used, so the cloud models are allowed, and the local models show what that approach costs.

Local models and privacy

Ollama runs open-weight models on the analyst’s own computer, and ellmer talks to it with chat_ollama(). Its advantage is privacy: in Ollama’s words, “Ollama runs locally. We don’t see your prompts or data when you run locally” (Ollama FAQ). A cloud model, by contrast, receives every prompt on the provider’s servers. The classification sends only the public code list, so the cloud models are allowed here, and the local models are a demonstration of the approach that text containing patient information would need. Whether a particular computer and setup are acceptable for protected health information is for the institution to decide, not something Ollama guarantees.

Two cautions about the privacy claim:

  • Ollama also offers cloud models, run on Ollama’s servers, and its cloud features are on by default. The models used here are local, but to rule out sending anything by mistake, Ollama can run in local-only mode: set the environment variable OLLAMA_NO_CLOUD=1 and restart Ollama; its log then shows Ollama cloud disabled: true (Ollama FAQ: disabling cloud features).
  • The models are downloaded from the internet (ollama pull), and Ollama checks for its own updates. Only the prompts and answers stay on the computer.

The disadvantage is setup. Ollama has to be installed, the models downloaded (5–8 GB each), and Ollama has to find a way to run them that works on that computer. On a computer without a capable graphics card the models run on the processor, which is much slower than the cloud models.

How to install Ollama and run the local models, and what to do if they crash, is on the code page: Setting up Ollama.

Agreement

A code is accepted when at least two of the three cloud models agree, a majority rule chosen for this demonstration (decided by Vagish Hemmige, 29 October 2026; the rule was three of five while the local models voted). Accepted codes are not read by a person. For codes with no majority, the deciders choose the category.

Fleiss’ kappa (Fleiss 1971) describes how far the models agree beyond chance: 1 is perfect agreement and 0 is what chance alone would give. It is reported in the agreement summary, and is not used to accept or reject codes.

What the local models showed

The two local models were meant to vote, and were run on the analyst’s laptop (processor only, 32 GB of memory) on 28 and 29 October 2026. Neither produced a complete, valid set of answers, so neither votes (decided by Vagish Hemmige, 29 October 2026); their answers are kept and reported, because what they did is itself a finding of this demonstration.

  • They dropped codes in longer batches. Both answered every code of a 5-code test. In batches of 25, Qwen left 150 of the 445 codes unanswered even after asking a second time, and Llama answered 7 of a 25-code test. Even in batches of 5, Llama left 124 codes unanswered on its first pass, and 28 after asking a second time.
  • They returned text that is not a code. Qwen put 25 codes in quotation marks and returned one answer with no code. Llama returned codes with </code> attached, the placeholders {code} and What is the code?, four invented web addresses, and codes not in the list (for example Z88.0, and D1022 for E1022). In all, 20 of its answers carried text that is not a code in the list, and it answered 6 codes more than once, some with different categories.
  • Some categories contradict the model’s own reason or are clinically wrong. Qwen put AIDS nephropathy (429) under Diabetes, with the reason that it “falls under viral/glomerular causes … not diabetes”. Llama put lupus nephritis (7100) and membranous nephropathy (5831) under Diabetes, and malignant hypertension (4010) under Diabetes with the reason “No indication of diabetes”.
  • They were slow. Qwen took 108 minutes for its answers; Llama about 2.5 minutes per batch of 5, close to four hours for the list. The cloud models took minutes.
  • A third model did not run at all. IBM’s Granite (granite4.2:8b) timed out after 30 minutes on a batch of 25 codes, and returned no answers for 5 codes in 15 minutes; Llama replaced it.

These are observations from one laptop and one prompt, not a general verdict on local models; larger local models, a computer with a capable graphics card, or a prompt asking for fewer codes at a time might do better.

How the mapping is checked

Agreement among the models shows only that they agree, not that they are right. The plan adds two checks on the data, run by an authorized person once the mapping is reviewed:

  • Against USRDS’s own grouping. The USRDS PATIENTS file carries a primary disease group for each patient (DISGRPC). A cross-tabulation of the six categories against it, as percentages with small cells suppressed, shows which codes the mapping puts somewhere USRDS does not. Differences are expected where the definitions depart from the CMS grouping on purpose (secondary glomerulonephritis, HIV, heart failure); any others point to codes to review.
  • Against the paper. The cohort’s share in each category, compared with the published Table 1 percentages above. A large gap in one category points to codes to review.

Both local models are small enough to run on a laptop. They show the approach that would be needed if a classification ever involved patient data: a model on the analyst's own computer sends nothing to an outside company. Here only the public code list is used, so the cloud models are allowed, and the local models show what that approach costs.

## Local models and privacy

[Ollama](https://ollama.com) runs open-weight models on the analyst's own computer, and ellmer talks to it with [`chat_ollama()`](https://ellmer.tidyverse.org/reference/chat_ollama.html). Its advantage is privacy: in Ollama's words, "Ollama runs locally. We don't see your prompts or data when you run locally" ([Ollama FAQ](https://docs.ollama.com/faq#does-ollama-send-my-prompts-and-answers-back-to-ollamacom)). A cloud model, by contrast, receives every prompt on the provider's servers. The classification sends only the public code list, so the cloud models are allowed here, and the local models are a demonstration of the approach that text containing patient information would need. Whether a particular computer and setup are acceptable for protected health information is for the institution to decide, not something Ollama guarantees.

Two cautions about the privacy claim:

-   **Ollama also offers cloud models**, run on Ollama's servers, and its cloud features are on by default. The models used here are local, but to rule out sending anything by mistake, Ollama can run in local-only mode: set the environment variable `OLLAMA_NO_CLOUD=1` and restart Ollama; its log then shows `Ollama cloud disabled: true` ([Ollama FAQ: disabling cloud features](https://docs.ollama.com/faq#how-do-i-disable-ollamas-cloud-features)).
-   **The models are downloaded from the internet** (`ollama pull`), and Ollama checks for its own updates. Only the prompts and answers stay on the computer.

The disadvantage is setup. Ollama has to be installed, the models downloaded (5–8 GB each), and Ollama has to find a way to run them that works on that computer. On a computer without a capable graphics card the models run on the processor, which is much slower than the cloud models.

How to install Ollama and run the local models, and what to do if they crash, is on the code page: [Setting up Ollama](classify_esrd_causes.qmd#setting-up-ollama).

## Agreement

A code is accepted when at least two of the three cloud models agree, a majority rule chosen for this demonstration (decided by Vagish Hemmige, 29 October 2026; the rule was three of five while the local models voted). Accepted codes are not read by a person. For codes with no majority, the deciders choose the category.

Fleiss' kappa [@fleiss1971kappa] describes how far the models agree beyond chance: 1 is perfect agreement and 0 is what chance alone would give. It is reported in the agreement summary, and is not used to accept or reject codes.

## What the local models showed {#what-the-local-models-showed}

The two local models were meant to vote, and were run on the analyst's laptop (processor only, 32 GB of memory) on 28 and 29 October 2026. Neither produced a complete, valid set of answers, so neither votes (decided by Vagish Hemmige, 29 October 2026); their answers are kept and reported, because what they did is itself a finding of this demonstration.

-   **They dropped codes in longer batches.** Both answered every code of a 5-code test. In batches of 25, Qwen left 150 of the 445 codes unanswered even after asking a second time, and Llama answered 7 of a 25-code test. Even in batches of 5, Llama left 124 codes unanswered on its first pass, and 28 after asking a second time.
-   **They returned text that is not a code.** Qwen put 25 codes in quotation marks and returned one answer with no code. Llama returned codes with `</code>` attached, the placeholders `{code}` and `What is the code?`, four invented web addresses, and codes not in the list (for example `Z88.0`, and `D1022` for `E1022`). In all, 20 of its answers carried text that is not a code in the list, and it answered 6 codes more than once, some with different categories.
-   **Some categories contradict the model's own reason or are clinically wrong.** Qwen put AIDS nephropathy (429) under Diabetes, with the reason that it "falls under viral/glomerular causes … not diabetes". Llama put lupus nephritis (7100) and membranous nephropathy (5831) under Diabetes, and malignant hypertension (4010) under Diabetes with the reason "No indication of diabetes".
-   **They were slow.** Qwen took 108 minutes for its answers; Llama about 2.5 minutes per batch of 5, close to four hours for the list. The cloud models took minutes.
-   **A third model did not run at all.** IBM's Granite (`granite4.2:8b`) timed out after 30 minutes on a batch of 25 codes, and returned no answers for 5 codes in 15 minutes; Llama replaced it.

These are observations from one laptop and one prompt, not a general verdict on local models; larger local models, a computer with a capable graphics card, or a prompt asking for fewer codes at a time might do better.

## How the mapping is checked

Agreement among the models shows only that they agree, not that they are right. The plan adds two checks on the data, run by an authorized person once the mapping is reviewed:

-   **Against USRDS's own grouping.** The USRDS PATIENTS file carries a primary disease group for each patient (`DISGRPC`). A cross-tabulation of the six categories against it, as percentages with small cells suppressed, shows which codes the mapping puts somewhere USRDS does not. Differences are expected where the definitions depart from the CMS grouping on purpose (secondary glomerulonephritis, HIV, heart failure); any others point to codes to review.
-   **Against the paper.** The cohort's share in each category, compared with the published Table 1 percentages above. A large gap in one category points to codes to review.
